Most inherited homes I see haven't been touched in years. Sometimes decades. The question families ask me almost immediately is: do we fix it up, or do we sell it the way it sits?

Probate Home Repairs Before Sale: Fix Up or Sell As-Is?

Most Probate Properties Need At Least Something

Deferred maintenance is the norm, not the exception. I walk into these homes and see peeling paint, worn carpet, kitchens that haven't been updated since the '80s. That's not a knock on the family. Life gets busy, people age, and things get put off.

The condition of the property shapes almost every decision that follows. Price, buyer pool, timeline, negotiations. All of it flows from what you're working with.

There's also the probate layer on top of it. Probate doesn't happen overnight. There's a court process involved, an executor with specific authority, and a timeline that doesn't always bend to what's convenient. All of that affects how you approach probate home repairs before sale. You're not just a homeowner deciding to paint the kitchen. Other people are involved, and the process has rules.

My starting point with almost every inherited home is the same. Clear out the personal items first, then see what you're actually dealing with.

What Fixing It Up Actually Gets You

My honest take after nearly 40 years in this business: most buyers want a turnkey home. They're stretching to buy in the first place. By the time they've scraped together a down payment and covered closing costs, there's not much left. They don't have a remodel budget sitting around. They want to move in and not deal with it.

That's who's looking at your property. A lot of them, actually. Compare that to the investor pool. Fewer buyers, and every one of them is coming in to lowball. They need room to make their profit, and they're not going to pay what a retail buyer would.

Estate property fix-up costs can feel like a lot upfront. What you're really buying is access to a bigger, more motivated pool of buyers. More buyers competing for the same home gives you real negotiating strength. When the home is turnkey, you're not negotiating from weakness.

Pre-listing repairs also cut down on inspection surprises. A lot of deals fall apart or get renegotiated after the inspector shows up. If you've already handled the obvious stuff, you've reduced that risk significantly.

As I tell my clients: when your home is in turnkey condition, you have much stronger footing in negotiations. That's not a sales pitch. That's just how it works.

What's Actually Worth Doing and What Isn't

Pre-listing repairs in probate need to be targeted. You're working with estate funds, and nobody wants to spend money on things that don't move the needle.

Paint first. Interior and exterior. It's the single highest-impact thing you can do. Fresh paint makes a tired house feel clean and current. It's not glamorous advice, but I say it first because it's true.

Landscaping and curb appeal come next. First impressions happen at the curb. Fix the front yard, trim what needs trimming. If the garage door is genuinely in disrepair, replace it. Buyers notice.

Flooring is worth addressing if the carpet is stained or the floors are genuinely damaged. Carpet or laminate, either works depending on the home. That one returns your money. I've seen it consistently.

Kitchens and bathrooms are where families want to go big, and I usually pump the brakes. Kitchens don't give a full dollar-for-dollar rate of return. What they need is a good cleaning and staging. Same with bathrooms, unless there's some funky pink tile or a pattern nobody's going to like. In that case, deal with it. A full bathroom remodel on an estate property is usually not the move.

The small stuff matters more than people think. Dripping faucets, an unstrapped water heater, missing smoke detectors. Fix all of that before you list, not after the inspector finds it. Those items show up in every report and hand buyers something to negotiate against you with.

The whole place needs to be dialed in. Yard cleaned up, windows washed. It needs to be sparkling and smelling very clean. That sounds basic, but it's what buyers remember.

Who Actually Pays for Repairs Out of an Estate

This is a question I get from families early in the conversation, and it's worth addressing directly.

Repair costs in a probate sale typically come out of estate funds. The executor has authority to authorize reasonable expenses that benefit the estate, and getting the home ready to sell qualifies as exactly that.

That said, it's not a blank check. Repairs need to be documented and defensible. Other heirs may have questions. Depending on the type of probate authority involved, larger expenditures might need court approval before work begins.

This is exactly why probate home repairs before sale require someone who understands more than just the real estate side. The process has legal and procedural layers that affect what you can spend, when you can spend it, and how decisions get made.

I've handled a lot of probate property sales. The families who have the smoothest experience are the ones who understand what they're working within before they start making decisions.

When Selling As-Is Actually Makes Sense

Selling inherited property as-is is a real option, and I'm not here to talk anyone out of it.

If the estate has no cash reserves and the heirs can't front repair costs, as-is may be the only path forward. Time pressure is real too. Carrying costs on a vacant property add up, and family dynamics sometimes demand a faster close, not a better one.

Selling as-is doesn't mean selling blindly. Pricing strategy matters. Disclosure matters. You still need to know what you're doing.

Going in knowing what to expect helps. You'll attract more investors than retail buyers, and the price will reflect that. That's the trade-off, and it's a real one. As I said earlier, there are far more buyers who want a turnkey home than investors looking for a deal. Going as-is narrows your pool significantly. Sometimes that's fine, but know what you're giving up.

If you're thinking through this and weighing your options, talking to someone with experience preparing a home for sale in this kind of situation is worth the conversation before you decide.

If you have time and money, prepping the property pays off. If you don't, let's talk through what makes sense for your specific situation.
